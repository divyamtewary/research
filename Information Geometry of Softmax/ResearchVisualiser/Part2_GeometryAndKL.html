Section 1

Big Question: What happens to other tokens when one logit changes?

In Part 1 we learned $\partial P_i / \partial \lambda_i = P_i(1-P_i)$ — the self-influence. But Softmax is a system: changing one logit affects all probabilities. The cross-derivative $\partial P_j / \partial \lambda_i = -P_i P_j$ quantifies this.

Intuition — Stealing Probability Mass

Imagine three tokens sharing a fixed pie of probability. If Token A's logit increases, Token A's slice grows. But the total must stay 1, so every other slice must shrink. The amount stolen from Token B is proportional to both A's probability and B's probability — hence $-P_A P_B$.

The cross-derivative is always negative. Increasing any logit steals probability from every other token. Softmax is globally coupled — no token exists independently.

Section 2

Big Question: How does probability mass flow between tokens?

When a logit changes, probability mass is redistributed across the entire distribution. The amount each competitor loses depends on its current probability — big competitors lose more.

Intuition — Water Displacement

Think of each token's probability as water in a set of connected vessels. If you pour more water into one vessel, water flows out of all others. But the amount that leaves each vessel is proportional to how much water was already there — large vessels lose more than small ones.

Section 3

Big Question: How do we measure distance between two behavioral states?

Instead of measuring how different two hidden vectors $\lambda$ and $\lambda'$ are, the paper asks: how different are the probability distributions they produce? The answer is the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence.

Intuition — Surprise as Distance

KL divergence measures how "surprised" you'd be if you expected distribution $P$ but received distribution $Q$ instead. If they're identical, surprise is zero. The more they diverge, the larger the KL value. Crucially, it's not Euclidean distance — it respects the curved geometry of probability space.

$$D_{KL}(P_\lambda \| P_{\lambda'}) = \sum_y P_\lambda(y) \left[\log P_\lambda(y) - \log P_{\lambda'}(y)\right]$$
Why not Euclidean distance? Consider two points on a sphere — the straight-line (Euclidean) distance cuts through the sphere, but the meaningful distance follows the curved surface. KL divergence is the curved-surface distance for probability space.

✦ Key Insight

KL divergence is not symmetric ($D_{KL}(P\|Q) \neq D_{KL}(Q\|P)$), and it's not a metric. But it's the natural way to measure behavioral change in probability space — and we're about to discover it has a remarkable geometric dual.

Section 4

Big Question: What is the gradient of the log-normaliser pointing at?

We know $\nabla A(\lambda) = P$. But there's a deeper interpretation: the gradient equals the expected output embedding $E[Y]$. This bridges the gap between calculus and geometry.

Intuition — Center of Mass

Imagine each output embedding $Y_j$ is a weight placed on a board. The probability $P_\lambda(j)$ is how heavy that weight is. The center of mass — where the board balances — is the expected value $E[Y] = \sum_j P_\lambda(j) Y_j$. The gradient $\nabla A(\lambda)$ points exactly to this center of mass.

Section 5

Big Question: What is the hidden geometry behind Softmax?

We started with KL divergence (a statistical concept). Through pure algebra, it transforms into a Bregman divergence (a geometric concept). This reveals that Softmax induces a curved, non-Euclidean manifold on the representation space.

Intuition — The Tangent Plane View

Imagine a convex bowl (the graph of $A(\lambda)$). At any point $\lambda$, the Bregman divergence measures the vertical distance between the bowl and its tangent plane at $\lambda$, evaluated at another point $\lambda'$. This is exactly what KL divergence computes — it's the "error" of using a linear approximation at $\lambda$ to predict the value at $\lambda'$.

The Derivation — KL → Bregman

Starting from the definition of KL divergence and substituting the Softmax probability formula:

$$D_{KL}(P_\lambda\|P_{\lambda'}) = \sum_y P_\lambda(y)\left[(\lambda-\lambda')^T Y_y - A(\lambda) + A(\lambda')\right]$$

Since $\sum_y P_\lambda(y) = 1$ and $\nabla A(\lambda) = \sum_y P_\lambda(y) Y_y$,

$$= (\lambda-\lambda')^T \nabla A(\lambda) - A(\lambda) + A(\lambda')$$

Rearranging gives the Bregman divergence:

$$\boxed{D_{KL}(P_\lambda\|P_{\lambda'}) = A(\lambda') - A(\lambda) - \nabla A(\lambda)^T (\lambda' - \lambda)}$$

What This Means

The discovery that KL divergence equals a Bregman divergence is profound:

  • Statistical → Geometric: A purely statistical distance (KL) has an exact geometric dual (Bregman).
  • Curved Space: The function $A(\lambda) = \log Z$ defines a convex potential that shapes the curvature of the representation space.
  • Not Euclidean: Hidden representations live on a curved information-geometric manifold, not in flat Euclidean space.
  • Interpretability Implications: Methods that assume Euclidean geometry (linear probes, PCA, cosine similarity) may miss the true structure.

✦ Core Result

Softmax does not merely convert logits to probabilities. It induces an entirely new geometry on the representation space — an information-geometric manifold whose natural distance is KL divergence, algebraically equivalent to a Bregman divergence governed by $A(\lambda)$.

$$\boxed{\text{KL Divergence} \;\cong\; \text{Bregman Divergence} \;\cong\; \text{Curved Geometry}}$$
